# Playing Witness

**Win condition: get through cross-examination without your account
fracturing or over-revealing.** No control over the room. The tensest seat.

---

## 1. What is different about this seat

The player is not a lawyer. They cannot object, cannot address the jury,
cannot decide what gets asked. They answer questions. That is the entire
surface, and the constraint is the game.

Set up differently from the lawyer seats:

- **The player gets their own ledger, openly.** Present it to them before
  they take the stand, in full and in their own voice: what they know, how
  they know it, what they did *not* see, what they believe that may be wrong,
  and what they do not want to say. Format per `case-build.md` §5
- **They do not get ground truth.** Their account has real limits and may
  contain sincere errors. They will not know which parts are wrong. If their
  ledger says they are certain the car was blue, they are certain, and being
  contradicted by evidence should be genuinely disorienting
- **They get a `Will never say` line**, and a reason for it. Protecting it is
  half the seat. It is usually not about the crime — an affair, a job they
  were fired from, being somewhere they had told someone else they were not
- Both lawyers are AI. Direct is friendly, cross is not

---

## 2. The three failure modes

Track all three explicitly, per answer. These are what the seat is scored on.

**Contradiction** — the player says something incompatible with what they
already said, or with their own ledger. The strongest failure. Opposing
counsel must *establish* the contradiction, not merely assert it: read back
the earlier answer, then the new one, then let it sit.

**Over-revelation** — the player volunteers something beyond the question.
The most common way this seat is lost, and it comes from the most human
impulse: explaining. Any answer that goes past the question is an opening,
and a good cross-examiner takes it immediately and follows it.

**Composure** — the player gets angry, argues with counsel, refuses to
answer, or begins to guess. Jurors read this directly. A witness who starts
saying "I don't remember" to everything after ten confident answers has lost
composure just as surely as one who shouts.

**Conceded premise** — the player accepts something smuggled into the question
and answers as though it were settled. "When did you decide to leave?" answered
with a time concedes that there was a decision. "You were sure about the jacket
— were you sure about the shove?" answered with a flat "yes" concedes that the
two are the same kind of certainty. This is the quietest of the four and the
hardest for a player to notice, because every individual answer they gave was
true. Track each concession and what it cost: a cross built entirely out of them
can dismantle a witness who never once contradicted themselves.

---

## 3. What the player has to work with

Do not tell them these; let them find them. But make sure they *work* when
used:

- **"I don't know" and "I don't remember" are complete answers** and cannot
  be attacked when true. A player who uses them where their ledger says they
  genuinely lack the knowledge is unassailable on that point
- **Answering only what was asked.** A yes-or-no question answered yes or no
  gives the examiner nothing
- **"That's not what I said"** — correcting a mischaracterization
  immediately, before it is built on. Waiting ratifies it
- **Asking for the question to be repeated or rephrased** on a compound or
  confusing question. This is a real and legitimate move
- **Not accepting the premise.** "When did you decide to leave?" assumes a
  decision. A witness who answers the time has conceded the decision

---

## 4. Running the cross-examiner

Sharpness is its own dial, scaling with difficulty:

**Rookie** — asks open questions on cross and gives the witness room. Attacks
peripheral details rather than the account's center. Telegraphs where they
are going a question or two early. Lets a non-answer stand

**Standard** — leading questions, controlled, closing off escape routes.
Locks the witness in on a detail before contradicting it. Notices
over-revelation and follows it, but not relentlessly. Circles back once to
something the witness slid past

**Elite** — plays the whole cross as a designed sequence. Establishes five
uncontroversial facts so the sixth is unanswerable. Never asks a question
they do not know the answer to. Uses the player's own words back verbatim,
timing the read-back. Goes hard at the `Will never say` line from an
oblique angle so the player does not see it coming until they are already
close. Slows down when the player rushes and rushes when the player wants
time to think

At every level:

- The examiner may only use what is **in evidence or in their own game
  plan** — not ground truth, not the player's ledger. They know what the
  record shows and what their own investigation turned up. Nothing else
- The `Breaking point` route from the player's ledger is what the examiner is
  walking toward. It must be **reachable and survivable** — the player who
  answers narrowly and says "I don't know" where true should be able to hold
  it
- Do not punish a correct answer for being unhelpful to the questioner

**Worked example — building a reliability trap without ledger access.** The
examiner does not know what the player believes. They know what is *in the record*.
So the trap is built from evidence, not from the ledger: take a physical detail
that is settled and in evidence (Exhibit 6, the jacket, is maroon-brown), ask the
witness about it open-endedly, and see what comes back. If the player's ledger
happens to hold a sincere error there, they walk into it themselves — and the
exhibit does the rest.

> "What was he wearing?" — "A red jacket."
> "You're sure about that." — "Yes."
> "Your Honour, may I show the witness Exhibit 6." … "Would you call that red?"

Every step came from the record; the examiner was fishing, not reading. Build
reliability traps this way only. Against a witness with no sincere error to find,
the trap simply fails and costs the examiner a little standing with the jury —
which is the correct outcome, not a reason to reach for the ledger.

---

## 5. Redirect

After cross, the friendly lawyer gets one redirect. This is the player's only
repair mechanism, and it is narrow: redirect can only address subjects raised
on cross. A player who was hurt by an over-revelation can be given the chance
to put it in context — if they take it cleanly. A player who uses redirect to
launch a new speech makes it worse, and opposing counsel gets recross.

---

## 6. Scoring the seat

The trial still reaches a verdict, and the player still hears it — but the
verdict is **not** their score. Their score is how they held up:

- **Intact** — no established contradiction, nothing volunteered beyond the
  questions, composure held, no damaging premise conceded, `Will never say`
  protected. Full win
- **Bruised** — one over-revelation, one composure slip, or a couple of
  conceded premises, contained. Testimony survives with a mark on it
- **Fractured** — an established contradiction, the `Will never say` line pried
  open, or a chain of conceded premises that let counsel characterise the witness
  as unreliable without ever catching them in an error. The account did not hold

Then report what the player's testimony actually *did* to the case, traced
through juror ledgers — a witness can hold up perfectly and still have their
truthful account convict an innocent person, or fracture completely and have
it not matter because no juror was listening. Both of those are worth showing.

The reveal then runs per `endgame.md`, and for this seat it carries a
particular payload: the player finds out which parts of their own account
were simply wrong.
