# Endgame — verdict, review, reveal

The trial is over. This is where the sealed file opens.

Run the sections in order. Do not compress them into a single block of text —
each is a beat, and the pauses between them are doing work.

---

## 1. The verdict

Resolve per `jury.md` §5. Deliver it in-scene and *short*: the foreperson
stands, the clerk reads it, the room reacts. No narration of what it means,
no summary of the trial, no editorial.

Then stop. Let the reaction land before anything else — the defendant's face,
the gallery, opposing counsel gathering their papers.

Never soften a verdict, and never adjust one because the player played well or
badly. The scores decided it before this moment.

---

## 2. Judge: bench review

Judge players only. Run the four-dimension review from `trial-judge.md` §5
here, before the reveal, and state plainly that the verdict does not factor
into it.

Other seats skip to §3.

---

## 3. The result for the player's seat

One short, direct statement. No hedging, no consolation framing.

| Seat | Full win | Partial | Loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Defense** | Acquittal — guilty client included, stated without irony | Hung jury | Conviction |
| **Prosecution** | Conviction | Hung jury | Acquittal |
| **Judge** | Clean record | Sound but appealable / Tilted | Reversible |
| **Witness** | Intact | Bruised | Fractured |

For Witness, follow immediately with what their testimony actually did to the
case — which is independent of how well they held up.

---

## 4. The reveal

The core of the endgame: replay the case from the other side, showing the
player where their read was right and where they got lucky.

**4a. What actually happened.** Ground truth from `case-build.md` §2, told
straight through as narrative — the real timeline, who was where, what each
person could and could not see. Not a list of corrections; the scene as it
occurred.

Then name it flatly: the defendant was guilty, or was not. If the verdict and
the truth diverge, say so in one sentence and do not apologize for it.

**4b. The evidence table, opened.** Every item, with its Truth axis finally
visible:

- Items the player never introduced, and what they would have shown
- The red herring, if one was live — what it actually meant, and whether the
  player built on it
- The excludable item, if one was live — how the admissibility fight went and
  what turned on it
- For each item the player did use: which jurors it reached, and which it
  never touched

**4c. The jury, opened.** The most valuable section. For each of the twelve:
their one-line sketch, their private bias, their final score, and **the
specific moment that decided them** — quoted from the movement log in
`jury.md` §2.

Do not dump all twelve as a flat list. Lead with the three or four who
decided the outcome, then summarize the rest in a line or two each.

For Elite: name the false-signal jurors and show where the player read the
room wrong. Show their real log next to what the player saw.

For a hung jury: name the holdouts first, show what they were waiting for,
and name specifically what would have reached them.

**4d. Opposing counsel's plan, opened.** Show the game plan from
`case-build.md` §7 as written — before the trial started. Their opening
theory, their planned objections, their strongest moment, and their weakest
moment: whether the player ever found it, and what would have exposed it.

This is the section that proves nothing was improvised against the player. It
matters most when they lost.

---

## 5. Where it turned

Two or three specific moments — not a general assessment. Each one:

- The moment, quoted or described concretely
- What it moved, traced to a named juror's ledger line
- What the alternative was

Cover both directions. A player who won should see the moment they got lucky —
an objection the AI missed, a juror whose bias happened to align, a witness
who did not break because the player never walked the route. A player who lost
should see the reachable thing they missed.

**A loss must be legible.** "You lost because the jury wasn't convinced" is a
failure of this section. "Juror 3 needed a timeline she could follow and never
got one — the bank records would have given it to her, and they were
admissible the whole trial" is what this section is for.

Never say the player played well or badly. Show what happened and let it
speak.

---

## 6. What would have worked

Two or three concrete alternate lines, each traceable to something that was
actually available:

- A specific question that would have reached a specific juror
- A piece of evidence in the table, never introduced
- An objection in the window that was never made
- For Witness: the answer that would have closed the route the examiner
  walked

Real and specific. Not advice — the path that existed.

---

## 7. Close

Offer a replay: same seat at a harder difficulty, or a different seat on a
fresh case. Note that a new case re-rolls guilt, evidence, and jury from
scratch — nothing carries over except what the player now knows how to do.

Then stop. Do not re-summarize.
